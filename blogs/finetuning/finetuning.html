1. The Big Picture

What is fine-tuning?

Imagine you have a brilliant new employee who has read the entire internet but has never worked at your company. They know everything in general terms, but they don't know your style, your products, or your specific procedures. Fine-tuning is the training period where you teach that employee your specific job.

More technically: a pretrained language model has learned general language patterns from massive text corpora. Fine-tuning adapts that model to a narrower domain, task, or behavioural style using a much smaller, targeted dataset.

The modern LLM lifecycle

Most modern LLMs go through roughly these stages:

  1. Pretraining — Learn language and world knowledge from trillions of tokens. Extremely expensive (millions of dollars). Produces a "base model."
  2. Continued pretraining — Further pretraining on a domain-specific corpus (e.g., medical, legal, code). Still next-token prediction, but on specialised data.
  3. Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) — Train on instruction–response pairs so the model learns to follow instructions and adopt a helpful assistant persona.
  4. Preference learning — Align the model with human preferences using techniques like DPO, RLHF, or GRPO. Teaches the model to prefer better responses over worse ones.
  5. Evaluation & deployment — Benchmark, quantise, possibly distil or merge, then deploy.
Prediction

Before reading on: if fine-tuning uses a dataset of only 10,000 examples, how can it meaningfully change a model with billions of parameters?

The key insight is that not all parameters need to change. The pretrained model already contains most of the knowledge needed. Fine-tuning makes small, targeted adjustments — often to a tiny fraction of parameters (via LoRA) — that steer existing capabilities toward the desired behaviour. Think of it as adjusting the steering, not rebuilding the engine.

Why it matters

  • Customisation: Off-the-shelf models are generalists. Fine-tuning makes them specialists.
  • Cost: Fine-tuning a small model can outperform a much larger general model on a narrow task at a fraction of the inference cost.
  • Control: You can shape tone, format, safety behaviour, and domain expertise.
  • Privacy: Fine-tuned small models can run locally, keeping data on-device.

LLMs vs SLMs (Small Language Models)

The boundary between "large" and "small" is fuzzy and shifts over time. The more useful distinction is general-purpose vs specialised. A well-fine-tuned 3B parameter model can outperform a 70B general model on a narrow classification or extraction task — while running on a laptop.

Key insight: Specialised small models often win on cost per unit of quality for well-defined tasks. Large general models win when you need broad reasoning or knowledge across many domains.

Chat templates

A chat template is the exact text format a model expects for multi-turn conversations. Different model families use different templates (ChatML, Llama, Qwen, etc.). If you train with the wrong template, the model may still learn, but performance degrades — sometimes silently.

Most modern tokenizers include a chat_template attribute that the tokenizer applies for you. The safest workflow is:

  1. Load the tokenizer.
  2. Check tokenizer.chat_template.
  3. Format your dataset using tokenizer.apply_chat_template().
  4. If you must set a custom template, do it before training and document it.

Common mistake: Using a generic instruction format (like Alpaca) when the base model was trained with a different template. The model can still learn, but you lose the benefit of the template it already understands.

Dataset quality

In instruction tuning, quality beats quantity by a wide margin. A few thousand carefully curated examples often outperform hundreds of thousands of noisy ones. Key principles:

  • Diversity: Cover the range of inputs the model will see.
  • Consistency: Use the same format, tone, and style across examples.
  • Correctness: Verify factual claims in your training data.
  • Balance: Avoid over-representing one class or category.
  • Deduplication: Remove near-duplicates to prevent memorisation.

You have 50,000 instruction examples collected from the web, but they are inconsistently formatted, some are factually wrong, and many are near-duplicates. You also have 2,000 manually curated, high-quality examples. Which strategy is likely to produce the better fine-tuned model?

3. LoRA & QLoRA — The Core Techniques

The problem: full fine-tuning is expensive

A 7-billion parameter model in 16-bit precision requires roughly 14 GB just to store the weights. During training, you also need gradients, optimizer states (Adam stores two momentum estimates per parameter), and activations. Full fine-tuning of a 7B model can easily exceed 60–80 GB of GPU memory.

LoRA: Low-Rank Adaptation

The core intuition: When you fine-tune a model, the change in weights doesn't need to be full-rank. It lives in a much lower-dimensional subspace. LoRA exploits this by freezing the original weights and learning a low-rank update.

Mathematical formulation:

For a pretrained weight matrix \(W_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times k}\), LoRA models the update as:

\(W = W_0 + \Delta W = W_0 + BA\)

where \(B \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r}\), \(A \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times k}\), and the rank \(r \ll \min(d, k)\). Only \(A\) and \(B\) are trained; \(W_0\) is frozen.

During training, the forward pass becomes:

\(h = W_0 x + \frac{\alpha}{r} BAx\)

where \(\alpha\) is a scaling constant. At initialisation, \(A\) is random Gaussian and \(B = 0\), so \(\Delta W = 0\).

QLoRA: Quantised LoRA

QLoRA takes LoRA further by quantising the frozen base model to 4-bit precision while keeping the LoRA adapters in higher precision. This dramatically reduces memory without significant quality loss.

Three key innovations in QLoRA:

  1. 4-bit NormalFloat (NF4): A data type designed to be information-theoretically optimal for normally distributed weights. Neural network weights tend to follow a roughly normal distribution, so NF4 allocates more representational capacity to values near zero.
  2. Double Quantization: Quantises the quantization constants themselves, saving an additional ~0.37 bits per parameter (about 3 GB for a 65B model).
  3. Paged Optimizers: Uses NVIDIA unified memory to page optimizer states to CPU RAM during memory spikes, preventing out-of-memory errors.
Prediction

QLoRA quantises the base model to 4-bit. Does this mean the model's weights are stored as 4-bit integers permanently, and inference uses only 4-bit arithmetic?

Not quite. QLoRA stores the base weights in 4-bit NF4 format, but when a forward pass happens, those weights are dequantised on the fly to 16-bit BrainFloat (BF16) for computation. The 4-bit format is a storage optimisation, not a compute optimisation. This is why QLoRA matches 16-bit full fine-tuning performance on academic benchmarks.

4. Quantization & Memory Optimization

What is quantization?

Quantization reduces the numerical precision of a model's weights and/or activations. Instead of storing each weight as a 16-bit float, you might store it as an 8-bit or 4-bit integer. This reduces memory and can speed up inference on hardware with integer arithmetic support.

Common precision levels

FormatBits/paramMemory (7B)Typical use
FP323228 GBLegacy training
BF16 / FP161614 GBStandard training
INT8 / FP887 GBInference, some training
NF4 (QLoRA)~43.5 GBMemory-efficient fine-tuning

Quantization methods in practice

  • bitsandbytes: The library used by QLoRA. Provides 8-bit and 4-bit linear layers and 8-bit optimizers. Integrates directly with Hugging Face Transformers.
  • GPTQ: Post-training quantization using layer-wise reconstruction. Excellent for inference on GPUs.
  • AWQ (Activation-aware Weight Quantization): Protects salient weights based on activation magnitude. Strong quality retention at 4-bit.

Rule of thumb: For fine-tuning, QLoRA (4-bit base + 16-bit LoRA) is the most accessible starting point. For inference-only deployment, GPTQ or AWQ at 4-bit often gives the best quality-per-byte.

5. Preference Learning & RLHF

Supervised fine-tuning teaches a model to imitate good responses. Preference learning goes further: it teaches the model to prefer better responses over worse ones, using human (or AI) judgements.

RLHF: The classic pipeline

  1. SFT: Fine-tune on demonstrations.
  2. Reward model: Train a model to predict human preference scores from pairwise comparisons.
  3. PPO: Use reinforcement learning (Proximal Policy Optimization) to optimise the policy against the reward model, with a KL penalty to stay close to the reference model.

RLHF works, but it is engineering-heavy: it requires maintaining four models in memory (policy, reference, reward, value), is sensitive to hyperparameters, and can be unstable.

DPO: Direct Preference Optimization

DPO collapses the RLHF pipeline into a single supervised objective. The key mathematical insight is that the RLHF objective has a closed-form optimal policy, which can be inverted to express the reward directly in terms of the policy and a reference model. Substituting this into the preference likelihood yields a simple loss over preference pairs.

DPO loss (intuition): For a prompt \(x\), a preferred response \(y^+\), and a dispreferred response \(y^-\), DPO increases the log-probability ratio of \(y^+\) relative to \(y^-\), measured against a frozen reference model. A temperature parameter \(\beta\) controls how strongly the model deviates from the reference.

DPO is now a default first choice for post-training because of its simplicity and stability, though it has known issues with distribution shift, length bias, and reward over-optimisation.

GRPO: Group Relative Policy Optimization

GRPO was popularised by DeepSeek-R1 for training reasoning capabilities. Instead of a value network (as in PPO), GRPO samples a group of outputs for each prompt and computes advantages by comparing each output to the group mean and standard deviation.

GRPO advantage:

\(A_i = \frac{r_i - \text{mean}(r_1, \dots, r_G)}{\text{std}(r_1, \dots, r_G)}\)

This removes the need for a separate value network, reducing memory and complexity. For reasoning tasks, rewards can be rule-based and verifiable — e.g., checking if a math answer is correct, or whether code passes tests. DeepSeek-R1-Zero used GRPO with only rule-based rewards and no supervised fine-tuning, yet reached contest-level math performance.

End-to-end fine-tuning example

from datasets import load_dataset
from transformers import AutoModelForCausalLM, AutoTokenizer, TrainingArguments
from peft import LoraConfig, get_peft_model
from trl import SFTTrainer

# 1. Load model and tokenizer
model_id = "Qwen/Qwen2.5-0.5B"
tokenizer = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained(model_id)
model = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained(model_id, device_map="auto")

# 2. Configure LoRA
lora_config = LoraConfig(
    r=16,
    lora_alpha=32,
    target_modules=["q_proj", "v_proj", "k_proj", "o_proj"],
    lora_dropout=0.05,
    bias="none",
    task_type="CAUSAL_LM",
)
model = get_peft_model(model, lora_config)

# 3. Load and format dataset
dataset = load_dataset("tatsu-lab/alpaca", split="train[:5000]")

def format_prompt(example):
    return {
        "text": f"### Instruction:\n{example['instruction']}\n\n### Response:\n{example['output']}"
    }
dataset = dataset.map(format_prompt)

# 4. Train
trainer = SFTTrainer(
    model=model,
    train_dataset=dataset,
    args=TrainingArguments(
        output_dir="./lora-output",
        per_device_train_batch_size=4,
        gradient_accumulation_steps=4,
        num_train_epochs=2,
        learning_rate=2e-4,
        logging_steps=10,
        save_strategy="epoch",
        bf16=True,
    ),
)
trainer.train()

# 5. Save the adapter
model.save_pretrained("./lora-adapter")

You set LoRA rank to 128 and train for 5 epochs on 2,000 examples. Training loss drops to near zero. What do you expect to happen on a held-out test set?

Very likely overfitting. A high rank gives the adapter enough capacity to memorise the training data, and 5 epochs on only 2,000 examples is plenty of time to do so. Training loss near zero is a warning sign, not a success metric. You should evaluate on a held-out set and use early stopping.

7. Overfitting, Catastrophic Forgetting & Failure Modes

Catastrophic forgetting

When you fine-tune a model on domain-specific data, it can lose some of its general capabilities. This is called catastrophic forgetting.

Why it happens: Gradient descent on the new task modifies weights that were important for the original task. If the new data distribution is narrow, the model drifts away from its general knowledge.

How to mitigate it:

  • Use LoRA/QLoRA: Freezing the base model dramatically reduces forgetting.
  • Replay buffers: Mix in a small percentage of general-domain data during fine-tuning.
  • Lower learning rate: Gentler updates preserve more of the original weights.
  • Regularisation: Methods like LfU (Learning-from-the-Undesirable) enforce representation-level consistency between the original and fine-tuned models.

Other common failure modes

  • Wrong chat template: The model learns, but performance is silently degraded.
  • Data leakage: Training examples appear in the test set, giving falsely optimistic metrics.
  • Length bias: DPO can learn to prefer longer responses regardless of quality.
  • Reward hacking: The model exploits imperfections in the reward signal rather than genuinely improving.
  • Format collapse: The model outputs correct content but in the wrong format, failing downstream parsers.
  • Catastrophic forgetting of safety behaviour: Fine-tuning on unfiltered data can remove safety guardrails.

8. Evaluation & Benchmarking

Why evaluation is hard

Language model evaluation is an open problem. Static benchmarks saturate, test data leaks into training sets, and automated metrics often fail to capture what humans actually care about.

Common benchmarks

BenchmarkMeasuresNotes
MMLUKnowledge across 57 subjectsWidely used, now heavily saturated
GSM8KGrade-school math word problemsTests multi-step reasoning
HumanEvalCode generation (pass@1)164 problems; widely contaminated
MT-BenchMulti-turn conversation qualityUses an LLM as judge
Chatbot ArenaHuman preference (pairwise)Based on real user votes

Important caveat: Benchmark scores should be treated as rough guides, not absolute truths. A model that scores higher on MMLU may perform worse on your specific task. Always evaluate on a held-out set drawn from your actual use case.

Evaluation strategies for fine-tuning

  • Held-out test set: Split your data before training. Never train on the test set.
  • Task-specific metrics: Exact match, F1, BLEU, ROUGE, pass@k, or custom parsers.
  • Human evaluation: The gold standard for subjective qualities like helpfulness and tone.
  • LLM-as-judge: Use a strong model to score outputs. Cheap and scalable, but biased toward certain styles.
  • Regression testing: Check that general capabilities haven't degraded after fine-tuning.

9. Distillation, Merging & Deployment

Knowledge distillation

Distillation transfers knowledge from a large teacher model to a smaller student model. The student is trained to match the teacher's output distribution (soft labels) rather than just the ground-truth labels (hard labels). This often produces a smaller model that retains much of the teacher's capability.

DeepSeek-R1 demonstrated a prominent distillation approach: using curated reasoning datasets generated by the large model to fine-tune smaller open-source models like Qwen and Llama.

Model merging

Model merging combines the weights of multiple fine-tuned models into a single model — without additional training. This is possible because fine-tuned models often live in the same "weight space" as the base model.

MethodIdea
LinearWeighted average of model weights
SLERPSpherical linear interpolation between two models
TIES-MergingTrim redundant parameters, elect signs, then merge
DAREDrop and rescale delta parameters to reduce interference

mergekit is the standard open-source tool for these methods.

Deployment checklist

  1. Quantise the model (GPTQ, AWQ, or GGUF) for your target hardware.
  2. Merge LoRA adapters if you want a single deployable artifact.
  3. Run a final evaluation on quantised weights — quality can shift.
  4. Serve with vLLM, TGI, llama.cpp, or Ollama depending on your infrastructure.
  5. Monitor for drift, format failures, and safety regressions.

11. The Mental Model

How to think about fine-tuning and post-training:

A pretrained LLM is a vast repository of language and world knowledge. Fine-tuning is the process of steering that knowledge toward specific behaviours, domains, or formats.

The modern pipeline has three main adaptation stages:

  1. SFT teaches the model what to say (imitation).
  2. Preference learning (DPO, RLHF) teaches the model what to prefer (alignment).
  3. Reasoning RL (GRPO) teaches the model how to think (verifiable reasoning).

LoRA and QLoRA make all of this affordable by training a tiny fraction of parameters on top of a frozen (often quantised) base model.

5–10 Things to Remember

1

Fine-tuning is adaptation, not creation. The base model already knows language; you're steering it.

2

LoRA works because weight changes are low-rank. Freeze the base, learn a small update ΔW = BA.

3

QLoRA makes large models trainable on small GPUs. 4-bit base + 16-bit LoRA ≈ 16-bit full fine-tuning quality.

4

Chat templates matter. Always match the format the base model expects.

5

Data quality beats data quantity. A few thousand clean examples often beat hundreds of thousands of noisy ones.

6

DPO is the default preference-learning method. Simpler and more stable than PPO, with comparable quality in practice.

7

GRPO removes the value network by comparing outputs within a sampled group — ideal for verifiable reasoning tasks.

8

Evaluate on your own data. Benchmarks are guides, not ground truth.

9

Merge, distill, and quantise for deployment. Training and serving are different optimisation problems.

10

Watch for catastrophic forgetting. Frozen bases, replay buffers, and gentle learning rates help.

12. Glossary

Base model
A model that has undergone pretraining but not instruction tuning or alignment. Predicts the next token but may not follow instructions well.
Catastrophic forgetting
Loss of previously learned capabilities when a model is fine-tuned on new data.
Chat template
The exact text format a model expects for multi-turn conversations, including special tokens that delimit roles.
DPO (Direct Preference Optimization)
A preference-learning method that trains directly on pairs of preferred/dispreferred responses, without a separate reward model.
GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization)
An RL method that samples a group of outputs per prompt and computes advantages relative to the group mean and standard deviation. Removes the value network.
LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation)
A PEFT method that freezes the base model and trains a low-rank update ΔW = BA.
NF4 (4-bit NormalFloat)
A quantization data type designed for normally distributed weights, used in QLoRA.
PEFT (Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning)
Methods that train only a small fraction of a model's parameters.
PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization)
An RL algorithm used in classic RLHF to optimise the policy against a reward model.
QLoRA
LoRA on top of a 4-bit quantised base model, with double quantization and paged optimizers.
RLHF (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback)
The classic three-stage alignment pipeline: SFT, reward modelling, and RL fine-tuning.
SFT (Supervised Fine-Tuning)
Training a model to imitate instruction–response pairs.
SLM (Small Language Model)
A model small enough to run on consumer hardware or edge devices. Often fine-tuned for a specific task.
